Programmatic Video Editing

Five narrated explainers, each made by code. Three are cut from real, openly licensed footage, and two are animated from storyboard files in Blender and Unreal Engine, over public-domain pictures and maps. Every graphic is drawn in code and timed to the narration, and none of the video is AI-generated. The narrators are Gemini text-to-speech voices.

The Battle of Mactan

For a history channel · 60 seconds · 1080p, with a 9:16 cut

Where the facts come from

  • Antonio Pigafetta's eyewitness account, in Blair and Robertson, The Philippine Islands, vol. 33
  • Wikipedia, Battle of Mactan
  • Republic Act 11040, which made 27 April Lapu-Lapu Day
  • National Geographic, on the 18 who finished the voyage

Pictures and sound

Six public-domain pictures from Wikimedia Commons, each captioned on screen. They include a 16th-century engraving of the fight, a map from a manuscript of Pigafetta's account that marks where Magellan died, and Visayans drawn in the Boxer Codex about 1590. The 14 sound effects are from Freesound. Three are CC BY: "Regular Arrow Shot with rattle" by brendan89 (CC BY 3.0), "Deepest Boom Yet" by hykenfreak (CC BY 4.0) and "Steampunk Crossbow Shot 1" by qubodup (CC BY 4.0). CREDITS.md lists every file with its source.

Drawn by code, with no footage

One Python storyboard file says where every shape, line of text and picture sits on each of the 1,440 frames, and both Blender and Unreal Engine render that same file. One dot stands for 10 men, so the landing shows 6 dots for Magellan's 60 men against 150 for more than 1,500 warriors, as Pigafetta counted them.

The 9:16 cut

For Shorts and Reels, on the same timing and soundtrack. The text is larger, the timeline runs down the frame, and everything stays clear of the captions and buttons the apps lay over the bottom of the frame.

The same storyboard in Unreal Engine

Unreal draws the shapes as unlit basic meshes and the pictures as flat textures, seen through a long lens so the view is nearly flat. Its colours match Blender's to within 2 or 3 RGB steps.

One cue from the code

EFFECTS += [(f(852) - 1.5, ARROW, 0, 2.7, -10), (f(852) + 0.05, IMPACT, 0, 0.3, 0)]

The arrow's whistle starts 1.5 seconds early, so it strikes as the ring around Magellan's dot appears at frame 852.

Checked

The storyboard checks its own counts (150 warrior dots, 6 Spanish dots, and 9, 4 and 15 dead), that no picture is stretched, and that nothing leaves the frame. Frames from every scene of each render were reviewed. The narration was first recorded line by line, which sounded like ten different reads and clipped some word endings, so the whole script was read again in one take. Whisper heard all 123 narrated words in the final mix (it wrote the numbers as digits and "crews" as "cruise"), which plays at -14.7 LUFS. Its first read-back heard "waiting for them" as "waiting for him" under the musket fire, so the last two shots were moved to after the line.

The Battle of Balete Pass

For a history channel · 88 seconds · vertical, for Shorts and Reels

Where the facts come from

  • Robert Ross Smith, Triumph in the Philippines (US Army, 1963), the Army's official history
  • Commonwealth Act 679 (1945), which renamed the pass for General Dalton
  • Republic Act 10796 (2016), which made it a national shrine
  • The national historical marker at the pass (2005)

Pictures and sound

Four US Army Signal Corps photos, Dalton's West Point portrait and two maps from Smith's history, all public domain and each captioned on screen, and a 2016 photo of the shrine by Patrick Roque (CC BY 4.0), credited on screen. One photo carried a typed wartime caption with a slur, and the crop leaves it out. The sound effects are from Freesound; two are CC BY: "Distant Battlefield Soundscape" by Kostas17 (CC BY 3.0) and "amazon 04.wav" by volivieri (CC BY 4.0). CREDITS.md lists every file with its source.

Facts checked against the sources

The dates, the casualty figures, the heights and the renaming were checked against Smith's text and the laws themselves. Figures the sources can't support were left out: a memorial plaque's totals survive in only one transcription, and a casualty count on Wikipedia isn't in the pages it cites. The screen says what each bar counts: the US figures are the 25th Division's three infantry regiments, and the Japanese figure is the 10th Division's dead, from 21 February until the pass fell.

The same storyboard in Unreal Engine

The lines drawn over the maps are rectangles turned to the angle between two points, and both engines draw them the same way.

One cue from the code

ROUTE5 = [(340, 1447), (374, 1371), (322, 1305), (324, 1282), (359, 1223), (387, 1192)]
ring(m19(*ROUTE5[2]), (372, S2[1]))

Route 5's corners are points on the Army's map, in its own pixels, and the third is the map's pass symbol. The ring around it pops at frame 372, where the narrator says "Balete Pass".

Checked

The storyboard checks that the three bars share one scale, that every map point falls on its map, that no picture is stretched and that nothing leaves the frame. Every render was reviewed at four frames a second. Whisper heard all 211 narrated words in the final mix (it wrote the numbers as digits and "the Second" as "II"), which plays at -14.2 LUFS.

How each video is made

  1. Claude Code writes the script from cited sources and records where every figure comes from.
  2. Gemini TTS reads the whole script in one take. Whisper reads every take back before anything is built on it. That read-back caught a line that sounded like its opposite and a mispronounced word, and both were recorded again.
  3. faster-whisper times every word of the voiceover.
  4. timeline.py turns those times into the cut. Each cut lands 0.12 seconds before the word that opens its phrase, on a whole frame.
  5. footage.py searches Wikimedia Commons, Pixabay and Openverse for openly licensed clips, photos and sound, skips anything marked as AI-generated and keeps each file's credit. Every clip is still checked by eye: the pyramid searches turned up 3D renders and a UFO scene.
  6. Manim draws every graphic on a transparent layer, from counters and charts to maps and diagrams, and each one starts on a word. The Great Pyramid video also has a second cut built in Remotion, where the graphics are React components on the same timings.
  7. FFmpeg adds a slow push-in to each still, lays the graphics over the footage, and mixes the voice, music and effects to -14 LUFS, with the music ducking under the voice.
  8. Contact sheets of every build, a Whisper read-back of the final mix and a design audit catch what's left before a cut is called done.

The rule that places every cut, from timeline.py:

LEAD = 0.12  # a cut lands this far ahead of the word that opens its phrase

def before(word, n=1):
    """The cut for the phrase that opens with this word, on a whole frame."""
    return frame(at(word, n) - LEAD)

Change a word in the script, record it again, and the cuts and graphics move with the new timings.

The two battles are animated instead of cut. Their scripts and voices go through steps 1, 2 and 8, but a storyboard file sets every frame, Blender and Unreal Engine render it, and sound.py places each line, effect and stretch of ambience on the storyboard's frame numbers. For Balete Pass the narration came first, and the storyboard was laid out on its measured lines.
