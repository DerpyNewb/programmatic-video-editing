Programmatic Video Editing

Sixteen narrated explainers, each made by code. Thirteen are animated from storyboard files in Blender and Unreal Engine, over public-domain pictures, maps and real terrain, and seven are in 3D, four of them with whole cities built from OpenStreetMap. Two of those seven are company histories, of SM and Jollibee. Three are cut from real, openly licensed footage. Every graphic is drawn in code and timed to the narration, and none of the video is AI-generated. The narrators are Gemini text-to-speech voices.

The Battle of Manila

For a history channel · 118 seconds · vertical, for Shorts and Reels · 3D in Unreal Engine · Watch on YouTube

Where the facts come from

  • Robert Ross Smith, Triumph in the Philippines (US Army, 1963), the Army's official history, chapters XII to XVI
  • Dale Andrade, Luzon, the US Army's campaign brochure (CMH Pub 72-28)
  • Reports of General MacArthur, Vol. I, chapter IX, "The Mindoro and Luzon Operations"

One cue from the code

# the crossing (926: "assault boats"): a bridgehead at Malacanan Gardens
{'show': (926, 1080), 'paint': [('japan', _SOUTH), ...,
                                ('us', _oval(14.5915, 120.9972, 0.0055, 0.0062))]},

Each phase of the areas held is a list of shapes, painted in order and shown between two frames. At frame 926, as the narrator says "assault boats", a small American foothold appears on the south bank of the Pasig at Malacañan Gardens, where Smith puts the crossing. Each phase fades into the next over 16 frames.

How this one was made and checked

The city, pictures and sound

The city is today's: about 195,000 building footprints from OpenStreetMap, drawn low to stand in for the 1945 city, with ten buildings that stood in 1945 at their own height, among them the Legislative Building, Manila City Hall and San Agustin Church. Land reclaimed from the bay since the war is drawn as sea. The ground is elevation data from AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen; SRTM and GMTED2010 from the US Geological Survey, ETOPO1 from NOAA), rendered at true scale under Unreal's physical sun, sky and volumetric clouds. The four photos are from the US Army Signal Corps, the US Navy and the National Archives, all public domain and captioned on screen. The unit symbols are drawn with milsymbol (MIT). The sound effects are from Freesound; eight are CC BY: "040313_INS Quatre Cantons_Ciutat del Futur.wav" by sonsdebarcelona, "M1A2 Abrams Accelerating.wav" by flyguy85, "fire_embers_large_campfire.aif" by Dynamicell, "building_collapse04_enclosed_space.wav" by onteca and "Regular Sniper Rifle Shot [w/ Bolt Reload] (Outdoor/Mixed)" by EFlexMusic (CC BY 3.0), and "Church Bells, Distant, A.wav" by InspectorJ, "Campfire.wav" by el-bee and "Church Bell" by bone666138 (CC BY 4.0). CREDITS.md lists every file.

Who held what

A tint shows who held which ground, the way strategy-game maps do: blue for the Americans, hatched amber for the Japanese, and a bright front line where the two meet. It is traced from Smith's three maps of the battle, with their dated front lines of 7, 12, 18 and 22 February, and simplified, so the screen calls it approximate. Manila is flat, so the tint is drawn by finding where each pixel's line of sight meets the ground, and it lines up with the Unreal render without rendering the city again.

Facts checked against the sources

Every date, number and quote was checked word for word against Smith, the Army brochure and MacArthur's reports. Where they differ, the video follows Smith: he ends the battle on 3 March, and the brochure on 4 March. Five districts turn to ruins as the narration reaches them, the ones Smith's summary lists as wrecked (p. 307), so Ermita and Malate stay standing although the fighting went through them. Smith warns that the casualty figures are uncertain, and the screen says so. The explosions mark where fighting was, not where any shell landed.

Checked

Stills along the camera's flight and contact sheets of the composed frames were checked before each render; the 3D takes 7 minutes in Unreal once the city is imported. A check in the storyboard fails any title, label or picture that strays under the strips and buttons that Shorts and Reels draw over a video. Whisper read the final mix back, writing the numbers as digits and some names by ear, such as "malakanyan" for Malacañan. The mix plays at -14.3 LUFS.

The Battle of Baguio

For a history channel · 112 seconds · vertical, for Shorts and Reels · 3D in Blender · Watch on YouTube

Where the facts come from

  • Robert Ross Smith, Triumph in the Philippines (US Army, 1963), the Army's official history, chapters V, XXIV and XXV
  • Louis Morton, The Fall of the Philippines (US Army, 1953), for 1941
  • Reports of General MacArthur, Vol. I, chapters IX and XIV, and Vol. II, Part II, compiled from Japanese records

One cue from the code

TOP, BOTTOM, RIGHT, BUTTONS = 710, -480, 476, (350, 60)
check_safe(ELEMENTS, FRAMES, (-504, BOTTOM, RIGHT, TOP), BUTTONS, 'baguio HUD')

Positions count from the centre of the 1080 by 1920 frame. Text stays below the top 250 pixels and above the bottom 480, where Shorts and Reels draw the title, caption and channel name, and in the lower half it stays clear of the buttons on the right. Running the storyboard fails on any fixed title, label or picture that strays.

How this one was made and checked

The town, pictures and sound

Baguio's mountains are elevation data from AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen; SRTM and GMTED2010 from the US Geological Survey, ETOPO1 from NOAA), with heights drawn 1.5 times true, and its 15,760 buildings are today's OpenStreetMap footprints, drawn low to stand in for the 1945 town. Blender's EEVEE renders them under a physical sky and an afternoon sun. The six pictures are National Archives aerials, Signal Corps photos and two photos from Smith, all public domain and captioned on screen. The unit symbols are drawn with milsymbol (MIT). The sound effects are from Freesound; two are CC BY: "Pine forest birds in Maclear, Eastern Cape" by Vortichez and "Swoosh" by EminYILDIRIM (both CC BY 4.0). CREDITS.md lists every file.

Who held what

A tint shows who held which ground: blue for the Americans and Filipinos, hatched amber for the Japanese, and a bright front line where the two meet. It is traced from Smith's Maps 19 and 20 and simplified, so the screen calls it approximate. Blender lays it on the terrain and the roofs, so ridges hide it the way they hide the ground. Where no map gives a line, the sources file says what the tint guesses. Volckmann's guerrillas held much of the countryside north of Baguio, but no map gives its extent, so the tint leaves it out.

Facts checked against the sources

Every date, number and quote was checked word for word against Smith, Morton and MacArthur's reports. The Army's campaign brochure was left out, because it gets this front wrong in places: it calls Route 11 "the easiest road into the mountains", while Smith calls it the most strongly defended. The American cost isn't shown, because Smith gives no total for the drive on Baguio. Asin's place is approximate, and the screen says so. The explosions and fires stand for the artillery and the fighting, not a record of what was hit.

Checked

Quarter-size stills and contact sheets of the composed frames were checked before each render; the 3D takes 16 to 29 minutes in Blender's EEVEE, and the text overlay 7 to 12 more. The stills caught every burst and fire sitting at sea level, under two kilometres of mountain, until the storyboard measured their ground, and caught muted notes vanishing against bright clouds. Whisper read the final mix back, writing the numbers as digits and some names by ear, such as "bood" for Bued. The mix plays at -14.7 LUFS.

The Battle of Leyte

For a history channel · 112 seconds · vertical, for Shorts and Reels · 3D in Unreal Engine · Watch on YouTube

Where the facts come from

  • M. Hamlin Cannon, Leyte: The Return to the Philippines (US Army, 1954), the Army's official history
  • Reports of General MacArthur, Vol. I, chapter VIII, "The Leyte Campaign"
  • Charles R. Anderson, Leyte, the US Army's campaign brochure (CMH Pub 72-27)
  • Martin Waldman, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency", Naval History and Heritage Command
  • Republic Act 1745 (1957), which made 20 October a special holiday in Leyte

One cue from the code

aa.render_warm_up_frames = True
aa.render_warm_up_count = 32

The first full-size stills showed the islands as kilometre-wide facets. Unreal's Nanite loads fine terrain detail only after it has drawn a frame, and the render queue's warm-up frames weren't drawing. These two lines fixed it, and every frame now shows the full 75-metre elevation grid.

How this one was made and checked

Terrain, pictures and sound

The island is real elevation data from AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen; SRTM and GMTED2010 from the US Geological Survey, ETOPO1 from NOAA), with heights drawn four times true, rendered at true scale under Unreal's physical sun, sky and volumetric clouds. The roads follow OpenStreetMap. The three photos are MacArthur wading ashore (US Army Signal Corps, NARA 111-SC-407101), a Japanese destroyer sinking in Ormoc Bay (US Navy) and the 77th Division's convoy approaching Deposito (US Army, in Cannon), all public domain. The unit and ship symbols are drawn with milsymbol (MIT). The sound effects are from Freesound; four are CC BY: "Notification Chime" by hykenfreak, "click 1 d.wav" by TicTacShutUp and "ExplosionBombBlastAmbient6.wav" by zimbot (CC BY 4.0), and "Bass Drum Hit 1.wav" by CouleurCasquette (CC BY 3.0). CREDITS.md lists every file.

Facts checked against the sources

Every date and number was checked word for word against Cannon, MacArthur's reports, the Army brochure, the Navy's history and the law. Where they differ, the screen says which it follows: Cannon and the brochure date Ormoc's fall to 10 December, while the marker in Ormoc says 11 December. The Japanese losses are the US Army's estimates, and the screen says "by estimate". Leyte's length, the number of paratroopers and a single total of Japanese dead are left out, because the sources disagree. The explosions mark where fighting was, not where any shell landed.

Checked

Full-size stills along the camera's flight were checked before the full render, which takes 5.5 minutes in Unreal once the terrain is imported. Whisper heard every narrated word in the final mix, writing the numbers as digits and some names by ear, such as "Ormuk" for Ormoc. The read-back also caught explosions drowning out "men and 282 ships", and they now play quieter under those words. The mix plays at -14.3 LUFS.

The Battle of Bataan

For a history channel · 102 seconds · vertical, for Shorts and Reels · 3D in Blender · Watch on YouTube

Where the facts come from

  • Louis Morton, The Fall of the Philippines (US Army, 1953), the Army's official history
  • Britannica, "Bataan Death March", and its note on how many marched and died
  • National Museum of the US Air Force, "Bataan Death March"
  • Republic Act 3022 (1961), which made 9 April Bataan Day

One cue from the code

{'name': 'orion_bagac', 'color': 'us', 'draw': (972, 1018), 'gone': 1770,
 'width': 0.11, 'path': ORION_BAGAC},

The final line is a glowing tube draped over the terrain. It draws itself from frame 972 and reaches Bagac at frame 1018, as the narrator says "Bagac", then undraws at frame 1770, when the defense collapses.

How this one was made and checked

Terrain, pictures and sound

The peninsula is real elevation data from AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen; SRTM and GMTED2010 from the US Geological Survey, ETOPO1 from NOAA), with heights drawn 2.5 times true, as the screen says. The pictures are an Office of War Information photo of an operating room on Bataan (public domain) and the Mt. Samat Memorial Cross by RanieljoseMCastaneda (CC0). The sound effects are from Freesound; one is CC BY: "Distant Battlefield Soundscape" by Kostas17 (CC BY 3.0). CREDITS.md lists every file.

Facts checked against the sources

Every date and number was checked against Morton's text, the laws and the two Death March sources. The defence lines run through the places Morton names, placed with Wikidata, GeoNames and OpenStreetMap; the points between them are estimates, and the screen calls them approximate. The death tolls are disputed, so the screen gives each figure with its source: Britannica's best estimate for the march, perhaps 30 % too high, beside the USAF Museum's "as many as 11,000".

Checked

Each camera move was checked on quarter-size stills before the full render, which takes 16 minutes in Blender's EEVEE. Those stills caught labels cut off at the frame's edge and the flat edge of the elevation data showing on the high views; a coarser grid of all of central Luzon now runs out to the horizon. Whisper heard every narrated word in the final mix, writing the numbers as digits and the Philippine names by ear. The mix plays at -14.3 LUFS.

The Battle of Manila, 1570

For a history channel · 97 seconds · vertical, for Shorts and Reels · 3D in Unreal Engine · Watch on YouTube

Where the facts come from

  • Emma Helen Blair and James Alexander Robertson, eds., The Philippine Islands, 1493-1898, vol. 3 (1903): the "Relation of the voyage to Luzón", by someone on the expedition, and four other documents of 1570 to 1574
  • Francisco J. de Gamoneda, Plano de Manila y sus arrabales (1898), for the shoreline

One cue from the code

SHOTS = [cue(6, 'opened', 2 + 10 * k) for k in range(3)]  # the fort's guns, one after another (Relation, p. 100)
STORM = cue(7, 'stormed')
BURN = cue(7, 'fire')

cue(line, word) is the frame where the narrator starts that word in the take, measured by Whisper. The fort's three shots start as the narrator says "opened", ten frames apart, and the town starts to burn on "fire".

How this one was made and checked

The town, the shore and sound

No picture or plan of the 1570 town survives, so the town, the village across the river, the boats, the trees and every effect are illustration built in code, and the screen says so. Each house has a burnt twin, with charred posts and fallen roofs, that takes its place as the fire passes. The shore is traced from Gamoneda's 1898 plan, the oldest detailed survey found, because nearly all of the land reclaimed from the bay came after it. The ground is elevation data from AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen; SRTM and GMTED2010 from the US Geological Survey, ETOPO1 from NOAA) and the Pasig is from OpenStreetMap, rendered at true scale under Unreal's physical sun, sky and volumetric clouds. The sound effects are from Freesound; two are CC BY: "Medieval cannon shot" by engray (CC BY 3.0) and "Warcry Yarghh! - British Male" by theuncertainman (CC BY 4.0). CREDITS.md lists every file.

Who says what

Every account was written by the expedition or the colony, so the video says who claims what. The blame on Sulayman for the first shot is what prisoners told the Spaniards, the dead and captives are counted by the Spanish account, and Sulayman's own explanation of 1571 is given in his words as Riquel recorded them. The accounts disagree on the number of men, the guns taken and how many shots hit the ship, so the video gives none of those numbers. Only one account dates the fight, to 24 May, and its writer was not there, so the screen gives it as one account's day.

Checked

Every quote on screen was checked word for word against the source text by a script. The shore was fitted to today's map with six points that stand on both, Fort Santiago's tip and five old churches, to about 50 metres. Whisper read the take back, and the scenes were timed to its measured line starts and word times. The mix plays at -14.4 LUFS.

Where the facts come from

  • SM Prime Holdings, Integrated Report 2025, for the 89 malls in the Philippines and 9 in China
  • SM Prime, Our History, and SM Investments' own releases on Shoemart and the first department store
  • Forbes, 2019, and the Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2019, which quotes Henry Sy on the war
  • The Philippine Star on Megamall, the 1994 listing and the Mall of Asia's opening day

One cue from the code

RISE = {'north_edsa': cue(6, 'North'), 'megamall': cue(7, 'Ortigas'), 'moa': cue(9, 'Mall')}

Each mall is its own mesh, with its origin at its base, so it grows from the ground. It starts to rise on the frame the narrator says the word in quotes, and a beam and a pulse of light go up with it.

How this one was made and checked

City, photos and sound

The city is today's Metro Manila, about 400,000 buildings extruded from OpenStreetMap at their tagged heights, under Unreal's physical sun, sky and clouds. The malls rise at today's size, not their size when they opened, and the screen says the buildings are today's. Seven photos from Wikimedia Commons (public domain, CC0 or CC BY) carry their author and licence in their captions. Three of them are CC BY 4.0: "Carriedo Street from Carriedo station" by PhiliptheNumber1, "SM Megamall Side View 2" by Ganmatthew and "Interior of SM Mall of Asia (01)" by Wide Awake!. The sounds are from Freesound; three are CC BY 4.0: two "Suspense Whoosh/Transition" files by tyops and "Ambient Piano Music #4" by benpm. CREDITS.md lists every file. This is an independent explainer, not made or endorsed by SM.

Facts checked against the sources

Every claim was checked word for word against its page on the day the video was made, including pages that block scripts and a 232-page report read in the browser. SM's own releases put the first Shoemart on Carriedo Street (2015) and on Avenida Rizal (2022), so the video marks the corner where the two meet and says the address is unknown. Where sources disagree, as on Henry Sy's birth date or the year Shoemart became SM, the video gives none.

Checked

Whisper read the final mix back and heard all twelve lines. Where it differs from the script, it writes numbers as digits and hears names by ear, such as "C." for Sy and "Carioto" for Carriedo. The mix plays at -14 LUFS.

One cue from the code

'plaza': {'at': PLAZA, 'osm': [61876421], 'height_m': 125.0, 'rise': cue(12, 'Plaza')},

Jollibee Plaza rises on the word "Plaza". OpenStreetMap gives no height for it, so the storyboard uses 125 metres, the height the building's property listings give, and the sources file says where that number comes from.

How this one was made and checked

City, globe, photos and sound

Jollibee Center, Jollibee Plaza and a stand-in for the 1,000th Philippine store rise when the narration reaches them, and the lights are the stores OpenStreetMap maps today, not a count. Five are CC BY: "Jollibee Chickenjoy with Palabok" by Jeff and "Jollibee Hong Kong October 2017" by Hana Yamada (CC BY 2.0), and "Jollibee in Intramuros (2026-01-15)" and "Mang Inasal in A. Del Rosario Street (2025-06-29)" by Wide Awake! and "Jollibee, Scarborough Town Centre" by Glenn Samonte (CC BY 4.0). Two sounds from Freesound are CC BY 4.0: "Resturant Ambience Tandoori.mp3" by digifishmusic and "Notification Chime" by hykenfreak. This is an independent explainer, not made or endorsed by Jollibee.

Facts checked against the sources

Every fact was checked word for word against its source, with the company's filings read as text from their PDFs. Where sources disagree, the video says less: the company's own list gives both 1984 and 1985 for market leadership, so the line says "mid-1980s", and no country is called Jollibee's first abroad, because Singapore, Taiwan and Brunei are each called that somewhere.

Checked

Drawn by code, with no footage

One Python storyboard file says where every shape, line of text and picture sits on each of the 1,440 frames, and both Blender and Unreal Engine render that same file. One dot stands for 10 men, so the landing shows 6 dots for Magellan's 60 men against 150 for more than 1,500 warriors, as Pigafetta counted them.

The 9:16 cut

The same storyboard in Unreal Engine

Checked

One cue from the code

assert len(crossings(ROUTE, RIVER)) == 2, 'the route to the fort fords the river twice (Mastrilli, p. 264)'

The diagram is drawn from the accounts' words, so the storyboard checks it against them. The march forded the river twice, so running the storyboard fails if the drawn route crosses the drawn river any other number of times. Other checks keep the guns and the fort on one bank and the sea routes off the land.

  • Joaquín Martínez de Zúñiga, Historia de las islas Philipinas (1803), a later history, in its 1814 English translation

One cue from the code

ring((bx, by), (w(12, 27), S11[1]), 'flash', 30)            # "the bay of Bancusay"
chip('"the bay of Bancusay"', (bx + 26, by + 34), (w(12, 27) + 2, S11[1]))

w(line, word) is the frame where the narrator speaks that word, from Whisper's word times on the take. As word 27 of line 12 is spoken, a ring flashes on the old map where the channel is marked, and its label follows two frames later.

The name on the end card

Popular accounts name the fleet's leader Tarik Sulayman. No account written at the time names him. His leading role and his death come from Zúñiga's history of 1803, more than two hundred years later, and the name comes from later genealogies; no Spanish record gives it. The end card gives the name, and under it a larger disclaimer that the narrator reads in full.

How each video is made

  1. Claude Code writes the script from cited sources and records where every figure comes from.
  2. Gemini TTS reads the whole script in one take. Whisper reads every take back before anything is built on it. That read-back caught a line that sounded like its opposite and a mispronounced word, and both were recorded again.
  3. faster-whisper times every word of the voiceover.
  4. timeline.py turns those times into the cut. Each cut lands 0.12 seconds before the word that opens its phrase, on a whole frame.
  5. footage.py searches Wikimedia Commons, Pixabay and Openverse for openly licensed clips, photos and sound, skips anything marked as AI-generated and keeps each file's credit. Every clip is still checked by eye: the pyramid searches turned up 3D renders and a UFO scene.
  6. Manim draws every graphic on a transparent layer, from counters and charts to maps and diagrams, and each one starts on a word. The Great Pyramid video also has a second cut built in Remotion, where the graphics are React components on the same timings.
  7. FFmpeg adds a slow push-in to each still, lays the graphics over the footage, and mixes the voice, music and effects to -14 LUFS, with the music ducking under the voice.
  8. Contact sheets of every build, a Whisper read-back of the final mix and a design audit catch what's left before a cut is called done.

The rule that places every cut, from timeline.py:

LEAD = 0.12  # a cut lands this far ahead of the word that opens its phrase

def before(word, n=1):
    """The cut for the phrase that opens with this word, on a whole frame."""
    return frame(at(word, n) - LEAD)

Change a word in the script, record it again, and the cuts and graphics move with the new timings.

The eleven battles and the two company histories are animated instead of cut. Their scripts and voices go through steps 1, 2 and 8, but a storyboard file sets every frame, Blender and Unreal Engine render it, and sound.py places each line, effect and stretch of ambience on the storyboard's frame numbers. From Balete Pass on, the narration came first, and each storyboard was laid out on its measured lines. Bataan (in Blender) and Leyte (in Unreal Engine, under a physical sun, sky and clouds) are real landscapes in 3D, built from open elevation data. Manila (in Unreal Engine, at true scale) and Baguio (in Blender) add the cities' buildings from OpenStreetMap and tint the ground each side held, phase by phase, from the Army's battle maps. Manila in 1570 (in Unreal Engine) is an illustrated town, built in code, on the shore of an 1898 plan of the city. Corregidor, Irisan and Bangkusay Channel stack flat layers at different depths so the camera can move between them. The two company histories, SM in Unreal Engine and Jollibee in Blender, fly over today's Metro Manila from OpenStreetMap, and each building they name rises from the ground on the word that names it.
